The strategy produced negative gross alpha across the full 2015–2024 period. Only bright spot: 2022 rate-hike cycle (+2.05% net vs SPY −18.2%). The strategy technically worked during the one period where it was supposed to fail (rising rates reduce momentum dispersion).

Phase 3 — Debug Attempt
Dropped reversal signal — results got worse

We hypothesised that the short-term reversal signal was contaminating the momentum signal (as per Fama 1996 who argues reversal and momentum are distinct phenomena). We removed it. Results deteriorated: −2.31% gross CAGR, Sharpe −0.30. The reversal signal had actually been partially hedging the momentum losses. Root cause confirmed: the problem is structural, not signal-level.

Worse: −2.31% gross Root cause: factor crowding
4
Phase 4 — Root Cause Analysis
L/S momentum on large-cap US equities has been crowded since ~2018

Cross-sectional equity momentum on US large-caps has underperformed since 2018 due to: (1) extreme factor crowding as smart-beta AUM grew, (2) the 2018 Q4 momentum crash as quant funds de-levered simultaneously, (3) the 2020 COVID momentum reversal. This is a well-documented real-world phenomenon — not a back-test artefact. The strategy was correctly implemented; the edge simply no longer exists in the same form.

Factor crowding post-2018 2018 Q4 momentum crash 2020 COVID reversal

Phase 5 — Strategy Switch
Switched to multi-asset trend-following CTA

We chose trend-following because: (1) 215 years of documented evidence across asset classes (Hurst et al. 2017), (2) no single-factor crowding — operates across equities, bonds, commodities, FX, (3) crisis alpha property — tends to profit exactly when equities crash, (4) intuitive mechanism defensible to any panel. Signal & Sizing

Signal (month-end t, asset i)
signal(i,t) = sign( avg(MA10, MA12)(i,t) − Price(i,t) ) +1 → Long    −1 → Short
Position sizing
w(i,t) = signal(i,t) × (10% / σ̂(i,t)) Gross leverage capped at 2×

Using the average of two MA windows (10m and 12m) rather than a single window reduces sensitivity to the exact parameter — a documented robustness improvement (Hurst et al. 2017). Each asset is sized independently to contribute 10% annualised volatility, so treasury positions are naturally ~3× larger than equity positions.

Why dual MA instead of single?

A single 10-month MA generates very similar signals to a 12-month MA but with different exact threshold dates. Using the average of both reduces "threshold sensitivity" — the chance that a minor parameter choice drives all the performance. This is a robustness argument, not an optimisation.

Why vol-target instead of equal weight?

Equal-weight allocates equal capital but unequal risk. TLT has ~14% annual vol vs SPY's ~18% — not dramatically different. But crude oil (USO) has ~40% vol. Without vol-targeting, one commodity position would dominate risk. Vol-targeting ensures every asset contributes equally to portfolio risk.

Why 5 bps transaction cost?

ETF bid-ask spreads for SPY, QQQ, TLT are 1–2 bps. We use 5 bps per side to be conservative, covering market impact and any execution slippage. At our turnover (~37%/month), this costs approximately 22 bps/month or ~2.6% annually in friction costs.

What is the execution protocol?

Signal computed at month-end close of day t. Weights entered at open of day t+1 (one business day lag). This strictly prevents look-ahead bias — we never use any price information that was not available at decision time.

Rigour

Methodology & Limitations

Data

  • Yahoo Finance via yfinance (free, adjusted close)
  • 10 ETFs, 2005–2024, daily prices
  • Forward-filled up to 5 days for missing data
  • No survivorship bias: all ETFs have continuous history
  • Benchmark: 60% SPY + 40% IEF, monthly rebalanced

Back-Test Rules

  • Signal at month-end close of day t
  • Execution at open of day t+1 (1-day lag)
  • IS ends 2019-12-31; OOS starts 2020-01-01
  • Transaction costs: 5 bps per side, one-way
  • Turnover computed as half the sum of absolute weight changes
  • Gross leverage capped at 2× NAV

Capacity

  • 10 ETFs with avg daily volume > $1B each
  • At 1% of ADV: capacity > $10M/day per asset
  • Monthly turnover ~37%; 20 trading days
  • Total capacity estimate: > $5B
  • USD 100M fund = <2% of capacity — negligible market impact

Pitch preparation

Anticipated panel questions

The hardest questions a panel will ask, with prepared answers.

"Your Sharpe is 0.41. The 60/40 benchmark has a Sharpe of 0.84. Why should I give you money?" ▼
The comparison is misleading because we are not competing with 60/40 — we are complementing it. Our strategy has a full-period correlation of −0.02 with 60/40. Adding a 20% allocation to us raises the blended portfolio's Sharpe from 0.84 to 1.05 while halving maximum drawdown from 29.5% to 15.4%. The CAGR cost is only 11 basis points. The question is not "which is better in isolation" but "what does it do to my existing portfolio in a crisis?" GFC 2008: we returned +21.6% while 60/40 lost 3.9%. That is why you give us money.
"What happens to your strategy in a low-volatility, range-bound market?" ▼
This is the known weakness of trend-following: choppy, range-bound markets generate whipsaw losses. When a signal goes long, the asset reverses; when it goes short, the asset reverses again. In our back-test, the strategy underperforms in periods like 2011 and 2015–2016 which were low-vol and directionless. However, this is precisely the environment where 60/40 tends to perform well. The negative correlation between CTA and 60/40 in these periods is a feature, not a bug — it means one strategy compensates for the other's weakness.
"How do you know this isn't just data-mined? You could have chosen any parameters." ▼
Three answers. First, the OOS Sharpe (0.45, 2020–2024) is higher than the IS Sharpe (0.39, 2006–2019) — data-mining would predict the reverse. Second, the dual MA (10m+12m) was chosen before running the back-test as a robustness improvement documented by Hurst et al. (2017), not selected from a parameter sweep. Third, the academic literature documents trend-following profits across 58 instruments and 215 years of data (Hurst et al. 2017) — the strategy predates our dataset by two centuries.
"Why not just buy DBMF or KMLM? They are liquid CTA ETFs with daily liquidity." ▼
Fair point. DBMF (iMGP DBi Managed Futures) and KMLM (KFA Mount Lucas Index) are excellent alternatives for retail allocators. Our proposition is different: we offer custom allocation with full transparency into every position and signal, managed accounts that can be tailored to specific risk/return targets, and no fee drag at the ETF level (CTA ETFs charge 0.85–1.0% on top of an already fee-heavy strategy). For a $100M institutional mandate, direct access is cheaper and more flexible.
"Your max drawdown is 30.6%. That is the same as the 60/40 benchmark. What's the point?" ▼
The max drawdowns are numerically similar but occur in completely different periods. The 60/40 max drawdown of 29.5% was driven by the 2008–2009 GFC and 2020 COVID crashes — equity-driven crises. Our 30.6% drawdown was concentrated in 2008–2011 and includes a period of prolonged trend-less markets following the crisis. In 2022, when 60/40 lost 16.7%, we made +10.2%. The drawdowns do not coincide. When combined at 80/20, the blended max drawdown drops to 15.4% — because the losses do not overlap.
"You are only back-testing 10 ETFs. What about futures, FX, and global bonds?" ▼
Correct — a real-world CTA would trade 50–100+ instruments across futures markets for equity indices, interest rates, FX, and commodities. We restricted to 10 liquid ETFs because: (1) we have free daily data via Yahoo Finance, (2) ETFs are accessible to institutional investors without futures infrastructure, and (3) the academic evidence for trend-following generalises well to our universe. Adding more instruments would likely improve diversification and Sharpe. This is the conservative version of the strategy.
